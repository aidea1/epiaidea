The Denominator

Some people are counted.
Most are not.

In 2011, a young pharmacist leaves a rain-soaked house in Hyderabad and a mother who does not want him to go. He carries one stubborn belief into the field: if he counts the dead honestly, the world will act.

He is not a soldier and not a natural hero. He is a quiet, precise man who believes a number is a form of respect for the dead. Over the next eleven years — as a frontline epidemiologist across Africa and the Middle East — that belief is tested in the worst places on earth. It costs him almost everything.

Why the title
Every rate in epidemiology is a fraction. The numerator is the dead. The denominator is everyone who was supposed to be counted.

When a population is never enumerated, its deaths cannot become a rate — and what cannot become a rate does not become a policy. Below: 1,000 people. The lit marks are the ones a health system actually recorded.

Inside the Book

Memoir · 11 years · 3 continents

The Denominator is a field memoir about the unglamorous machinery of global health: the tally sheets, the refrigerated vaccine boxes, the census that was never taken, the outbreak line list assembled by candlelight. It follows the work that happens long before a number reaches a journal or a headline.

It is also an argument. Bhagavathula's career has been built on the premise that measurement is a moral act — that deciding who enters the denominator is a decision about whose life registers as a life. The book traces what happens when that premise meets institutions that would prefer a cleaner number, governments that would prefer a smaller one, and the ordinary exhaustion of a person who keeps insisting on the true one.

A number is a form of respect for the dead. Undercounting is not a technical error. It is a decision about who mattered.

Readers of Mountains Beyond Mountains, The Spirit Catches You and You Fall Down, and Atul Gawande's essays will recognise the terrain — but this book is written from inside the data, by the person holding the clipboard rather than the one interpreting it afterward.

About the Author

Akshaya Bhagavathula, PhD, PharmD, FACE

Professor of Epidemiology · Founder, EPIAIDEA

Trained as a clinical pharmacist in India before earning a PhD in epidemiology, Bhagavathula worked as a field epidemiologist with WHO, USAID, and UNICEF across Africa and the Middle East. He now researches digital epidemiology, pharmacovigilance, and geospatial health at North Dakota State University, and is a named collaborator on the IHME Global Burden of Disease study.
